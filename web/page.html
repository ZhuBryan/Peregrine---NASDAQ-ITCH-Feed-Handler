Peregrine

A feed handler that reads NASDAQ's raw order feed, rebuilds the order book for every listed stock, and hands best-price updates to a strategy thread over a lock-free queue.

Source on GitHub Built by Bryan Zhu

Everything here runs on one laptop (Ryzen 7 PRO 7840U) against NASDAQ's public sample data. The speed benchmarks ran under WSL2 with all 16 threads available; the full-day results ran in a 2-vCPU Linux virtual machine. Neither is tuned bare metal.

How it works

One thread owns the books and does all the work on the feed. It passes best-price changes to a second thread through a lock-free queue, so a slow strategy can never corrupt or block the book.

Performance

Two builds of the same program on the first 2 GB of the session (64.5 million messages): three runs each under WSL2, feed and strategy threads pinned to separate cores. The baseline swaps in the C++ standard library's containers.

Does the exchange keep its queue?

An exchange promises price-time priority: the best price trades first, and at one price the oldest order trades first. A second tool rebuilds every price level as a queue of individual orders and checks each of the day's executions against that promise.

What the audit found

  • Ranking orders by when their Add message arrives leaves 10,565 ordinary executions that appear to jump an older order, most of them in the half hour after the open.
  • Ranking by order reference number leaves none. Some orders reach the feed after a higher-numbered order at the same price, mostly just after the open, and the exchange trades them in number order.
  • The remaining out-of-turn executions are all of one message type: orders executing at a price better than the one they displayed, which fits their being ranked at that better price.
  • A replace that only shrinks an order behaves as a new order at the back of the queue.

This is one day of data, and the feed shows displayed orders only. The findings are what the data is consistent with, not rules quoted from the exchange.

Why the numbers can be trusted

Four independent checks, each of which fails on a decoding or bookkeeping bug.

  • Differential test. A random stream of adds, executions, cancels, deletes and replaces is compared, after every message, against a brute-force reference that rescans all live orders.
  • Unknown references. Any execution or delete naming an order the handler does not hold is counted. The full day had none.
  • End-to-end state. At the end of each run the strategy thread's view must equal the books for all 65,536 stock slots.
  • Network equivalence. Replayed over UDP with 2% of packets deliberately dropped, the output must match a file replay exactly: 732 packets dropped, 732 retransmit requests, 34,295 messages recovered.
